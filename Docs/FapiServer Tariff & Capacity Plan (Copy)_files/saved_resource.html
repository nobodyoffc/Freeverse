§1

What you are actually paying for

A FapiServer node runs five processes: Elasticsearch, a Freecash full node in Docker, FchParser, FeipParser, and the FapiServer itself on UDP 8500. Their cost profiles are nothing alike, and only two of them matter.

Memory — what you buy

Elasticsearch dominates. It wants half the box for heap and the other half for page cache. The Freecash node, both parsers and the FapiServer need another 5–6 GB between them. That sets a 16 GB floor for a single-box deployment, and makes RAM-per-dollar the number to shop on.

Egress — what scales

Every byte FUDP sends to a phone is metered somewhere. On AWS that is $0.09/GB; at a European value host it is $0.001/GB; on a dedicated box with an unmetered gigabit port it is free. Same bytes, an 80× spread. This single choice moves more money than every other decision in this document.

Storage — cheap and slow-growing

Freecash blocks are ~1 minute apart and mostly empty. 3.4M blocks is single-digit GB on disk; the ES indices built from them are ~15–30 GB with no replicas, growing well under 1 GB/month. Only the DISK and DOCK payload store grows with users, and that has its own meter.

Your time — the cost nobody invoices

An unmanaged VPS is cheaper on the bill and dearer in hours: no snapshots-as-a-service, no managed backup, hardware failure is your problem. At 10k users the invoice spread between hosts is 18×; after costing 2–5 hours a month at $50/hour it collapses to 2.6×. Both numbers are real; only the second one is decision-grade.

The gap between billed bytes and billed dollars

FAPI meters the FUDP payload. The host meters what leaves the network card. Those differ by a predictable factor:

FUDP header + crypto bundle …… 73 bytes per 1350-byte datagram
IP + UDP headers …………………… 28 bytes
ACKs, retransmissions, path probes … ~5–12% typical
────────────────────────────────────────
wire bytes ≈ 1.20 × billable payload bytes

Retransmitted bytes are deliberately not re-charged to the client, but the host charges you for them. Every capacity number below is stated in both units: billable (what you invoice) and wire (what the host invoices you).

The recommendation

Do not standardise on one host. Move bands as the server grows. Through T1 the invoice is noise — a $19 box and a $74 box are the same decision, so buy the one that costs you the fewest hours. From T2 the egress bill starts to matter and the value band earns its keep. At T3 an unmetered dedicated box for the data plane plus regional cloud edges is 4× cheaper than Lightsail and 14× cheaper than EC2, and by then the volume justifies the operational work.

What AWS is still worth paying for

Two things, regardless of where the server itself lives. Object storage for backups — the balance ledger, its WAL and the audit log belong in S3 or Backblaze B2 from day one, and that costs about a dollar a month wherever the compute runs. And Asia-Pacific reach, if Freer's users are there: Hetzner has one Singapore region with much worse traffic terms, while AWS, Vultr and DigitalOcean all have Tokyo, Seoul and Sydney. Latency and reachability for a UDP protocol are worth more than a cheap invoice.

§5

Setting the price

The price has to clear four hurdles in order: the marginal cost of the byte, the fixed cost of the box spread over the traffic, the referral shares skimmed off the top, and a margin that funds the reserve.

1 · marginal floor — the cost of one more gigabyte
hyperscaler $0.090/GB · bundled cloud $0.010 · value cloud $0.001 · dedicated $0.000

2 · add fixed cost, spread over billable traffic at target scale
price = ( all-in monthly cost ÷ billable GB ) × ( 1 + margin ) ÷ ( 1 − channel share )

3 · at T2, the median host, 30% margin, 20% consume channel
$379 ÷ 2,500 GB × 1.30 ÷ 0.80 = $0.246 / GB
…but the same sum on the cheapest T2 host gives $0.152 × 1.625 = $0.247
…and on Lightsail, $0.191 × 1.625 = $0.311

4 · round down to a defensible published number
pricePerKBOut = $0.20 per GB = $1.907 × 10⁻⁷ per KB

Rounding down from the arithmetic is deliberate. $0.246 is the price that recovers full cost at exactly 10,000 users; $0.20 recovers it at about 12,000 and leaves room to grow into. It is also 2.2× the hyperscaler marginal rate and 200× the value-cloud rate, which is the honest spread — most of what you are selling is a memory-heavy database and two parsers that run whether or not anyone queries them, not raw bandwidth.

Two adjustments follow. Inbound is free at every host, so ingress only needs to price the CPU and disk write an upload causes; 20% of the outbound rate deters abuse without punishing uploads. Stored bytes cost disk for as long as they sit there — $0.10/GB-month is above AWS gp3 at $0.08 and far above a Hetzner box where disk is bundled, which is the margin that pays for backing those bytes up.

Net revenue per user is $0.042/month. On the invoice alone the server is profitable from about 800 users. Counting your own time it is profitable from about 3,800, and genuinely comfortable past 10,000 — where the margin finally exceeds the effort by enough to justify the effort.

Because cost is a step function and revenue is a straight line, the server briefly goes underwater after every upgrade. That argues for upgrading late: the T1 box on a value host carries 20 TB of allowance, which is 65,000 typical users' worth of traffic. The real limit there is CPU and packets, not bandwidth, so let concurrency — not the bandwidth graph — trigger the move.

The referral shares are the second-largest cost

The current configuration sets orderViaShare to 30% and consumeViaShare to 20%. Applied together, a user acquired through a third-party channel hands over 50% of their gross spend — more than the infrastructure costs at every tier. Today this is harmless because defaultVia points at the operator's own FID and the share returns to you. It stops being harmless the moment real channels appear.

§7

Converting to FCH

The tariff above is in dollars because the invoices are. The service declaration wants FCH per KB. One formula bridges them:

satoshi per KB = 95.367 × ( USD per GB ) ÷ ( FCH price in USD )
FCH per KB     = satoshi per KB ÷ 100,000,000

the constant is 10⁸ ÷ 1,048,576 — satoshi per FCH, over KB per GB

Worked at four rates, for the recommended $0.20 / $0.04 / $0.10 tariff:

Repricing is not free, so the cadence matters more than the precision. The price lives in the on-chain service declaration, is read at server start or on a manual refresh, and is cached by every client. Review monthly, reprice only when the rate has moved more than 30%, and announce it in the app before the transaction lands. Between reviews the margin absorbs the drift, which is part of what the 30% is for.

Both directions hurt differently. If FCH falls, dollar revenue falls with it and you are underwater until you reprice. If FCH rises, you are overcharging in real terms and clients with a maxPayment guard — the FC-JDK client stops auto-recharge above 1 FCH by default — start refusing to top up. Same monthly discipline fixes both.

Three traps in the current config

The credit limit becomes one kilobyte. creditLimit is 10,000 satoshi. At any realistic price a brand-new user can afford roughly 1 KB before hitting 402 PAYMENT_REQUIRED. FapiBalanceManager logs a warning about precisely this at startup — do not ship past it. Note also that service.minCredit (in FCH) overrides settingMap.creditLimit (in satoshi), so raising only one of them does nothing.

home.api points at the Android emulator. 10.0.2.2 is the emulator's alias for the host loopback. Published on-chain, no real device can reach the service. This must be a public hostname before the declaration goes out.

minPayment is set for the old price. At 0.01 FCH it buys around 150 KB at the new tariff, triggering an auto-recharge transaction every few minutes. Size it to roughly a month of a typical user's traffic.

One code change worth making

FapiBalanceManager seeds its free list with PING, PONG, PEER_INFO and CHAT_ACK. map.register is not on it, and cost is computed as ceil(bytes/1024) × price — so a 250-byte NAT keepalive is billed as a full kilobyte, four times over, every 25 seconds. A user online three hours a day sends about 13,000 of them a month and is charged for 13 MB of traffic that was really 3 MB — roughly 5% of their bill, all of it for a packet that exists only to hold the firewall open.

Either add map.register to the default free set, or meter it in bytes rather than rounded kilobytes. The first is a one-line change and the honest one; the keepalive is infrastructure overhead, not a service the user asked for.

§9

Risks and other factors

The one that matters most

FCH revenue does not pay hosting invoices. Freecash trades on three markets with roughly zero daily volume. Whatever the tariff earns arrives as a token that cannot reliably be sold at any price. Plan the server as a dollar-funded operation with an FCH-denominated meter: hold a USD reserve covering 6–12 months of hosting, and treat FCH income as ecosystem credit — usable for paying other FAPI providers, funding DISK space, and settling with stakeholders and channels — rather than as revenue. Every profit figure in §6 is arithmetically correct and economically theoretical until FCH has a market.

Cheap hosts suspend relays

ROAD forwards opaque bytes between peers. That is a relay, and relays attract abuse complaints regardless of what is actually being relayed. Budget and value hosts act on complaints fast and ask questions later; hyperscalers have process. This is the single largest non-price risk in moving off AWS. Mitigate it with the 2-hop cap already in FAPI15, per-peer relay rate limits, a published abuse contact, and a rehearsed migration path to a second host.

Today's price is not next year's

Hetzner repriced twice in 2026 — up to 37% in April and 113–175% on the CPX and CCX lines in June. Oracle halved its free tier with no announcement and terminated instances above the new limit. Do not build a model that only works at one host's current sheet. The §5 sensitivity table exists so you can re-derive the price after any host's next increase.

Free credit is a Sybil target

Every new FID gets the credit limit, and FIDs are free to mint. Keep trial credit to a few hundred kilobytes, tie anything more generous to an FID with on-chain history, and rate-limit new-account creation per source address. At 150 KB per identity, a thousand fake identities cost you about three cents — which is the right order of magnitude.

UDP exposure

Port 8500 is open to the internet and answers unauthenticated packets. AWS gives you Shield Standard free; Hetzner and OVH include DDoS filtering; Contabo's is thinner. Whatever the host, add per-source packet-rate limits at the FapiServer and cap road.relay per peer per minute — the application-layer limit is the one you control everywhere.

The Docker image is from 2021

freecash v1.0.5 shipped February 2021 as a saved image archive, almost certainly amd64-only. That rules out ARM instances for the node — and ARM is where the cheapest RAM per dollar sits (Hetzner CAX41 gives 32 GB for $48). Verify with docker image inspect --format '{{.Architecture}}'. If it is amd64, either stay on x86 throughout, or run only the node on x86 and put ES and the FapiServer on ARM.

Where your users are

Hetzner has Germany, Finland, two US regions and Singapore — and Singapore has far worse traffic terms. If Freer's users are concentrated in Asia, the value band gets much less attractive and the bundled-transfer cloud (Vultr, DigitalOcean, Lightsail) wins on Tokyo, Seoul and Sydney presence. For a UDP protocol, reachability and RTT are worth more than the invoice difference.

The balance ledger is money

The LevelDB balance store, its WAL and the audit log are the record of what every user has paid. Losing them means losing balances and the ability to settle. Back them up off-instance daily to object storage, keep the audit log's hash chain intact, and test the snapshot → WAL → audit recovery path before you need it. This is the one piece of state a rebuild from chain cannot reconstruct — and it is the strongest argument for keeping one foot at a hyperscaler regardless of where compute lives.

Storage tiering for DISK

DISK payloads on AWS gp3 cost $0.08/GB-month; on S3 Standard the same blobs cost $0.023 and transfer to an in-region instance is free. On a value host, disk is bundled with the plan and effectively free until you exhaust it. Either way the bytes still leave through the FapiServer, so egress is unchanged — this only moves the storage line.

Commitment discounts

If you do stay on EC2, a 1-year no-upfront Compute Savings Plan takes roughly 30% off compute and a 3-year commitment roughly 50%. It does nothing for egress, which is the line that actually hurts. Lightsail, Hetzner and Contabo have no commitment discounts and need none; dedicated boxes carry a one-time setup fee instead, amortized above over 12 months.

On-chain running costs

Settlement runs every 14,400 blocks (~10 days) and broadcasts payouts; recharge detection needs a funded dealer address; changing the tariff is itself a transaction. Freecash fees are negligible, but the dealer needs a standing float — minDealerBalance is currently 0.01 FCH and should be sized to a few settlement cycles.

Competitors set the ceiling

Clients discover FAPI providers from the chain and can read every provider's price before connecting. There is no pricing power beyond what service quality justifies, and the recommended rate should be checked against what others publish before each repricing. Operating the reference implementation is an advantage in trust, not in price.

VAT and billing friction

European hosts quote ex-VAT. Customers outside the EU pay nothing extra; EU consumers without a VAT ID pay 19–20% on top, which erases part of the value-band advantage. Dedicated servers also carry a one-time setup fee — around $62 on the AX42 — and most value hosts require identity verification before they will sell you a large box.

§10

Operating rhythm

The model is only as good as its inputs, and three of them are guesses the server itself can replace within a month of real traffic: bytes per user, the wire-to-billable ratio, and peak concurrency.

Track monthly

  • Billable KB in and out, from EconomicsMetricsCollector — against wire bytes from the host's network counters. The ratio is your real overhead multiplier; the 1.20 used here is an estimate.
  • Cost per delivered GB, invoice and all-in. This is the number the price has to beat, and it should fall every month users grow.
  • Hours spent on the server. The most under-measured line in this document, and at every tier below T3 the largest one.
  • Revenue per MAU in FCH, and its dollar value at that month's rate. Two separate series; do not blend them.
  • Credit written off — accounts sitting at negative balance that never recharged. This is the true cost of the free tier.
  • DISK and DOCK GB-days stored against storage revenue, to confirm stored bytes pay for themselves.
  • Peak concurrent peers and packets/second, which is what actually triggers the next upgrade — not bandwidth.

Decide quarterly

  1. Reprice in FCH if the rate has moved more than 30% since the last change (§7).
  2. Re-derive the USD price if any host's sheet has changed materially, or if your own hourly rate has (§5).
  3. Upgrade when peak CPU passes 70% or ES heap pressure becomes routine — not when bandwidth runs out, which happens far later.
  4. Re-check the channel shares against actual third-party referral volume (§6).
  5. Re-measure ES and node disk against the projection, and resize before the volume is 80% full.
Sequence

Fix the config traps in §8 and the home.api hostname first — they block production regardless of pricing. Publish the tariff on the same on-chain update so clients see one coherent change. Run the pilot on the cheapest box that meets the §3 requirement, because at that scale the invoice is irrelevant and the only real cost is your attention. Then let the metrics above replace the assumptions in §3, and re-derive the price from your own numbers before the T1 upgrade.
